Nature-based care

What is nature-based occupational therapy?

Nature is not a cure-all. It can, however, become a meaningful and flexible setting for practicing the things you want to do.

By Kerri Newman-Darrow, OTR/L, CHT, RYT-200Updated September 20265-minute read

Occupational therapy uses purposeful activity to support health and participation. When the chosen activity takes place outdoors—or involves caring for, moving through, or connecting with the natural environment—it may be described as nature-based OT.

The important word is not simply nature. It is occupation: an activity that has meaning for the person and connects to a therapeutic goal.

What might nature-based OT include?

  • Walking on an accessible path to work on endurance, pacing, balance, or community mobility.
  • Gardening with adapted tools and joint-protection strategies.
  • Practicing transfers, equipment use, or safe movement for a return to outdoor recreation.
  • Using a sensory walk to practice attention, body awareness, or grounding.
  • Adapting hiking, paddling, or other valued leisure activities after illness, injury, or disability.
  • Creating a realistic plan for adding restorative outdoor time to a weekly routine.

The activity should fit the person. A nature-based session may be active or quiet, ambitious or very small. Sitting on a porch with intention can be as meaningful as covering a trail.

Why work in a real-world setting?

Outdoor environments provide genuine variation: uneven surfaces, changing light, weather, distractions, distance, and decisions about pacing. Those conditions can help a therapist assess and practice skills that may not appear in a predictable clinic.

They also give us access to activities people may genuinely want to resume. Motivation is different when the goal is not merely “stand for ten minutes,” but “work comfortably in my garden” or “walk to the overlook with my family.”

What does the evidence say?

Research on structured nature-based activities is promising, particularly for some adult mental-health and well-being outcomes. The evidence is still developing, study quality varies, and nature-based activity should not be presented as a replacement for appropriate medical or mental-health care.

At Kind Oasis, the outdoor setting is one possible context for skilled OT—not a universal prescription. Clinical reasoning, safety, accessibility, personal preference, and measurable goals still guide the plan.

Safety and accessibility come first

Before an outdoor session, we consider mobility, balance, endurance, cognition, vision, medication effects, weather, terrain, transportation, hydration, and emergency access. The location and activity can be modified, and indoor or telehealth alternatives remain available.

Nature-based care is not appropriate in every circumstance. Symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, acute shortness of breath, sudden neurologic change, or a new serious injury require medical evaluation rather than an outdoor therapy session.

A personal, not performative, approach

You do not need to identify as outdoorsy. You do not need special gear or a particular level of fitness. The starting point is your relationship with the activity: what feels meaningful, what feels possible, and what you hope to regain or discover.
